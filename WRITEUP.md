### How long did your first working version take on the full 50 files, and how long did your final version take? Which single change saved the most time? (100 words)
My first working version ran YOLO11l in FP32 on every frame. I never ran it on all 50 files: on a 96-video validation set (5,543 seconds) it took 1,659 s on two Kaggle T4s, projecting about 38 hours. My final version processed FP16 with adaptive sampling,and it processed all 50 files in 5 h 58 min. The biggest single saving was adaptive sampling: it cut the frames sent to the model from 27.8 to 8.6 per video-second, taking the FP16 projection of 15.5 hours to a measured 6 hours, about 2.6x. Switching to FP16 was a close second at 2.46x.

### Describe what your script does, step by step, from opening a Parquet file to writing an output line. (150 words)
run.py reads each Parquet file; each row is one video. It decode processes, one per spare CPU core, open the mp4 bytes with PyAV, apply any rotation tag and place each frame in a second: the floor of its timestamp minus the first frame's. Frames are converted to BGR, letterboxed to 640x384 as Ultralytics does, and written into shared memory, so frames are never copied through a queue. One GPU process per GPU batches 32 frames and runs YOLO11l in FP16, keeping the five classes at confidence 0.4 or above. For each second the model first sees 5 evenly spaced frames. If every object appears in at least 4 or at most 1, and at least 4 agree on the people count, the second is decided. Otherwise the remaining frames run and the brief's rule applies to all. Finished videos get their lines written; damaged ones get an error line.

### Which part of your final version takes the most time? How did you measure this? (150 words)
Running the model takes most of the time. In the full run, each GPU spent about 17,300 of 21,485 seconds (81%) on inference and 3,500 to 3,700 seconds (17%) idle, waiting for decoded frames. The pipeline measures this itself: each GPU process times its model calls and its waits for frames, and logs both totals every five minutes, along with frames inferred and seconds escalated (experiments/phase4_full_run/full_run.log). The idle time is new. In the every-frame runs, FP32 and FP16, the GPUs were idle for only 0.3 seconds, so decoding was not a limit. Once adaptive sampling cut the model's work to 8.6 frames per video-second, two decode processes on Kaggle's 4 vCPUs could no longer always keep both T4s busy. With more threads, I expect this wait to disappear and the model to be the only bottleneck, but I have not measured that.

### How did you check that your results stayed correct after you made the script faster? (100 words)
My reference was the first version: FP32 on every frame, on 96 videos from 12 files (5,543 seconds). tools/eval.py scores any output against it with the brief's three checks. FP16 on every frame scored object F1 0.9999, people exact 99.82%, within one 100%. tools/sweep.py replayed every sampling setting offline on the saved per-frame detections, so I knew each setting's cost and accuracy before choosing. The final settings scored 0.9993, 99.78% and 100%, well above 0.95, 85% and 95%. Unit tests check the median and second rules, and that my model input reproduces Ultralytics predict exactly on 1,320 real frames.

### Describe one suggestion from an AI tool that was wrong or that you decided not to use. How did you find out it was wrong, or why did you reject it? (100 words)
While planning, the AI told me decoding was cheap, about 8,500 frames per second, and YOLO was the bottleneck. That number came from an inspection script that only decoded frames. It never converted them from YUV to BGR, which YOLO needs. I questioned it, and the AI admitted it had not measured the conversion. Timing one video on my M2 Mac showed about 4,300 frames per second for decoding alone and about 1,200 with BGR conversion, so the conversion cost more than the decoding. That is why my design runs many decode processes and hands frames over through shared memory.


### If you had one more week, what would you change, and why? (100 words)
First, I would measure on an RTX 4090 with 32 threads. All my timings come from two T4s with 4 vCPUs, where the GPUs waited 17% of the time. Second, I would export YOLO11l to a TensorRT FP16 engine in the untimed preparation step. FP16 alone gave 2.46x, and TensorRT should add more, but I would check it against the reference first. Third, I would tune the batch size for the 4090; it is fixed at 32. Last, I would test damaged and unusual files, such as variable frame rate and rotated videos, which the brief warns about.
